What to notice

As ζ drops below 1 the poles become a complex-conjugate pair and the response overshoots and rings. At ζ = 1 (critically damped) the response is the fastest possible with no overshoot. Above ζ = 1 the poles split onto the real axis and the response becomes sluggish.

ωₙ scales the speed of everything: larger ωₙ pushes the poles further from the origin, shrinking rise and settling times while leaving the overshoot percentage unchanged.

G(s) is built from zeros and poles: Evaluating G at s = jω means measuring, from every zero and pole to the point jω on the imaginary axis, a vector — its length and angle. Multiply the zero lengths, divide by the pole lengths, and you get |G(jω)|, the factor by which a sine wave of frequency ω is scaled. Add the zero angles, subtract the pole angles, and you get ∠G(jω), the phase shift applied to that same wave.

What to notice

Raise ω with the slider: the pole vectors lengthen faster than the zero vectors, so |G(jω)| falls off. Move a pole's Re/Im close to jω and its vector shrinks — that term dominates the magnitude and its angle dominates the phase.

A complex-conjugate pair of poles or zeros always contributes two vectors, one from each partner — together they set both the magnitude and the phase, which is why complex poles bend a response in ways a single real pole cannot.
